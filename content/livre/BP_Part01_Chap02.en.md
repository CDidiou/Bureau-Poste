*(At the presbytery. The Priest is sitting by the window, polishing a small parish register with a cloth. The Midwife is wiping down the kitchen table. Two sharp knocks sound against the doorpost.)*

**The Cripple :**
Is it open to invalids, or must I come back on market day?

**The Priest :**
Sit down, Major, instead of standing there wearing out your good leg.

**The Cripple :**
Much obliged. *(He sits, placing his hat on his knees. His eyes linger for a moment on the Midwife's hands as she puts away the bottles.)* I hadn't seen you, Madame. Or rather… Mademoiselle. My respects. You take good care of our good priest.

**The Midwife :**
Good morning, Major. The care of souls and bodies takes no holiday.

**The Priest :** *(without looking up from his register, in an even voice)*
I was reading while I waited. The quiet here is good for reflection.

**The Cripple :**
Still buried in the Holy Scriptures?

**The Priest :** *(turning a page)*
Not only those. The diocese has just sent me the pre-war land records for the district. The bishopric's copies. They are sometimes more forthcoming than the ones at the town hall.

**The Cripple :** *(straightening slightly)*
Oh? And what do the bishop's papers have to say for themselves?

**The Priest :** *(in an almost detached voice, continuing to polish the leather of the register)*
They say that, since at least 1837, the Roche-Fontaine plot has had no right of way across the common. But more importantly… a marginal note in the parish records mentions a hiding place. A letter from the bishopric speaks of three sealed chests, buried beneath the corner marker. A plum tree.

**The Cripple :** *(a half-smile playing at the corner of his mouth)*
1837… The year of the patriots and the uprisings in Lower Canada. The notaries of the July Monarchy wrote with quill pens and couldn't tell a pistole from a louis d'or. One misplaced comma on a survey plan, Monsieur le Curé, and my ancestors' gold moves a metre north.

**The Priest :** *(amused, without looking up)*
Your ancestors had a long memory, Major. But the diocesan archives can see further than your shovels.

**The Midwife :** *(setting a steaming cup in front of the Major)*
Leave the notaries alone and drink this. And stop telling people at the inn that you move boundary markers with your wooden leg. The constable is a fool, but he has a tongue two metres long.

**The Cripple :** *(taking the cup. Their fingers brush for an instant; he stops, suddenly uncertain)*
Now, now… I couldn't possibly say things that never happened. You see, I'm in the middle of grafting some new fruit trees — young shoots. The village is badly in need of fruit, wouldn't you agree?

**The Priest :** *(in the same detached voice, continuing to polish the register)*
The town notary let slip that an educated man can make a deed of sale say almost anything he pleases. So many decent people still can't make head nor tail of contracts and their fine print…

**The Cripple :** *(fixing the Priest with a cold stare)*
Quite right, Monsieur le Curé. Education must do its work, and much faster than my fruit trees. As for me, I seek quarrels with no one. In any case, here are the accounts. *(He draws a folded sheet of paper from beneath his arm and lays it on the table.)* The record of herbal infusions for the women down in the lower district. I've entered everything as you asked. It's all in order.

**The Priest :**
So be it, Major. Heaven keeps the books.

**The Midwife :**
We're short of so many ingredients for the poultices… You have no idea. The needs are so great throughout the district. We are deeply in your debt, Major. All of us.

*(The Cripple looks at her for a moment. A heavy, unspoken silence settles between them beneath the Priest's watchful gaze.)*

**The Cripple :** *(quietly, struggling to his feet)*
Enough of the thanks. I've seen suffering. More than any one life ought to have to bear. And besides… if there were no one to read the labels on your bottles, you'd end up poisoning the constable for good.

**The Priest :** *(gently closing his register)*
That would be a great pity. The village would lose its only pair of eyes at night. Good day, Major.

**The Cripple :**
Monsieur le Curé… Mademoiselle.

*(He inclines his head slightly towards the Midwife, puts his hat on, and leaves, the steady clack of his wooden leg echoing across the stone at the entrance.)*
