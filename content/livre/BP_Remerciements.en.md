# The Post Office (A Novel from the de Bernard-Lilaz cycle)

## The work

History does not knock at the door: it comes in through the dispatch counter and the lying-in room. 1919. The First World War has just ended. But the district is holding its breath once more. As the men return from the front, wounded in body and closed off in spirit, an invisible threat crosses the river: Spanish flu. To organise the quarantine and disinfect the mail, the Administration takes possession of the village.

A requisitioned post office becomes the pulsing, pitiless heart of the world — a place where cresyl circulars, death notices, requisition orders and letters from notaries pile up. At the centre of the upheaval: a devoted, authoritarian midwife; a former soldier known as the Cripple, a sly operator obsessed with land registers and the ink of the State; an analytical priest, forever being summoned back to his bishopric; an overwhelmed village constable; and finally Mathurin, a deeply suspicious peasant.

But when a tragic night of childbirth shatters their illusions and seals the men's fate, the story takes a turn. Marie-Zéphyrine de Bernardy-Lilaz orchestrates a fascinating game of mirrors. Behind the great adventure at its heart — a treasure hunt linked to the French expedition to Lower Canada in 1837 and an old military crate — lies the intimate indictment of a reclusive woman, fighting the world's decay and the straitjacket of social roles through the absolute power of fiction.

## The author
![Photo by the author](/Bureau-Poste/images/Zephyrine_Ww1.png)

Rosalind Cucurrou, known by her pen name Zephyrine Pearl de Bernard-Lilaz, remains an enigmatic and fascinating figure in rural literature between the wars. For thirty years, she walked the muddy roads of the district, treating fevers, tending wounds and attending births through the upheaval of the Great War and the epidemic of 1918–1920.

Then, little by little, suffocated by the silence of men, the weight of tragedy and the abdication of those who should have carried the world alongside her, she withdrew into the silence of her great stone house, with its massive oak beams, living as a recluse until her final breath. In what appears to have been her only known novel, Rosalind casts a moving gaze upon humanity, love and the rigidity of social roles that hold men's hearts in check.

## Acknowledgments

To the carpenter, first. For the life tenancy, for the dry wood laid each morning on the stone floor, the hot soup slipped to me without a word, and for swearing upon the Cross never to open the lock of my chest until I had been carried to the grave. Without your faithful silence — a silence you owed me, as you know better than anyone — these stories would have ended as ashes in the fireplace.

To the priest of this district, less for the man himself than for his words — the ones that knew how to hear what I left unsaid.

To Philip. For your wooden leg, which made the flagstones of the post office tremble; for your false boldness, your imaginary acres, and your fear of showing your poor heart. You never knew how to love me except by making me check your herbal-remedy records. I forgive you.

And to all the children, who are not fortunate enough to have anyone make them listen to the song of the birds.

Zephyrine Pearl de Bernard-Lilaz, at her home, Lower District.
