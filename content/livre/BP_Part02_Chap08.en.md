*(Mathurin’s large kitchen, late at night. The fire has died down to glowing embers, but the whole room seems bathed in a light that no longer belongs entirely to any one candle — a diffuse glow, almost suspended, as though the celebration itself had begun to shine from within. The table is cluttered with glasses and empty bottles. The Priest, his cassock undone at the neck, has rolled up his sleeves.)*

*(The Village Constable is refilling an inkwell with the concentration of a diligent schoolboy. Philip holds the quill, with Zephyrine standing behind him, one hand resting on his shoulder.)*

**Father Mathurin :** *(refilling the Constable’s pipe with a broad grin, all trace of the anger of previous days gone from his voice)*
Add a line about the plum tree, Philip! Say that beneath the roots, the captain carved the district motto with the tip of his sabre — and that it belongs to me, body and soul, with no business of the commune poking its nose into it!

**The Brave Constable :** *(thumping his fist on the table, laughing, and nearly overturning the inkwell)*
And put in that the constabulary spent three days looking for the document and never found the way, because I’d moved the boundary stone at Pretty Girls’ Hollow by fifty toises!

**The Priest :** *(smiling, dipping a biscuit into his wine, his voice lighter than it has been since the barn)*
Do not clutter the parchment with too many worldly embellishments, gentlemen! Let us say instead that Providence veiled the eyes of the Prefecture’s bloodhounds, to preserve the innocence of our valleys.

**Zephyrine :** *(leaning over Philip’s shoulder, her voice filling the room with a gentleness that seems to make the candlelight flicker more brightly)*
Let them have their say, Philip. Fill it with all the romance of your maps, all the poetry of our nights, all the bravery of your ancestors. Let those who read this story a hundred years from now feel their hearts fill with wonder.

**Philip :** *(reading aloud again, his voice deep and full)*
“And so it was that Philip and Zephyrine, guided by the faith of the Priest, the loyalty of the Constable and the greatness of the farmer, having preserved the secret of the Patriots of 1837, were united by the sacred bonds of marriage…”

**The Brave Constable :** *(raising his glass high)*
To the legend of Philip and Zephyrine!

**Everyone :**
To the legend!

*(Philip lays down the quill, rises in one light movement — almost too light, as though he were a younger man than himself — and rummages through the back of the old oak dresser. He brings out a squat, dust-covered bottle with a yellowed label.)*

**The Brave Constable :**
Saints preserve us! Muscat! Where on earth did you find such a treasure?

**Philip :** *(winking at Mathurin)*
Mathurin and I have our secrets.

*(He pours. The golden liquid flows into the glasses with an almost unreal slowness, catching all the light in the room as though each drop held a little of the sunlight from a summer none of them had ever shared.)*

**The Priest :** *(his eyes shining)*
Ah, now that is the language of carefree days. Tonight, heaven has come down into our glasses.

*(They raise their glasses. The sound of crystal lingers longer than it should, like a sustained note. Mathurin turns towards the cradle, where the child — whose face, tonight, finally seems to have settled for good — lets out a soft sigh.)*

**Father Mathurin :** *(dreamily)*
To think there was no gold to dig up, no chest of louis waiting beneath the earth… And I’ve never felt so rich in all my life.

**Zephyrine :** *(with a radiant smile, though for a moment her eyes seem to search for something beyond the circle of light)*
Gold fades, Mathurin, and the earth is covered in snow. But this happiness will never leave us. We found the treasure on the very first night: it was loving one another, forgiving one another, and, against all odds, being happy together.

*(Philip raises Zephyrine’s hand to his lips. Around them, the kitchen gradually ceases to be quite a kitchen: the shelves fade into a soft darkness, the stone walls dissolve into a glow with no clearly defined edges, as though the whole setting had decided to retain only what mattered — the table, the glasses, the beloved faces.)*

**Philip :** *(a glass of muscat in his hand, a conspiratorial smile)*
You see? We did make it in the end, the two of us. We carved out the shape of our own story.

**Zephyrine :** *(laughing, a light, almost childlike laugh)*
Our own legend… Oh, my love.

They raise their glasses again. The chink goes on and on, almost musical, until it becomes a continuous note, like a thread stretched through the golden silence of the room.

Then, without knowing why, Zephyrine turns her head towards the back of the kitchen. There is a door there, half open — a door she cannot remember ever having seen in that place before. The light around her no longer flickers; it has become perfectly still, as though held in place.

In the doorway stands a figure, equally motionless. A woman. Zephyrine recognises her without the slightest doubt — the carpenter’s wife.

At that precise moment, Philip sets down his glass. He rises — slowly, steadily, as though drawn by a thread only he can feel. He says nothing. He looks neither at Zephyrine nor at anyone else. He crosses the room in measured steps, without hesitation, and passes through the doorway, which closes behind him without a sound.

Zephyrine remains standing, her glass still in her hand. She does not move. She does not call after him. Her eyes slowly fill with tears, and one tear, then another, slips silently down her face, without a single sob.

Around her, Mathurin, the Village Constable and the Priest have their arms around one another’s shoulders, their glasses filled to the brim with muscat, and continue singing merrily as though nothing had happened — as though the door, the figure, and the sudden emptiness beside Zephyrine had never existed.
